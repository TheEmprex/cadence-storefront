About

Built around when, not what.

Almost every supplement range is organised around ingredients. You buy a magnesium product, a collagen product, a greens product, and then you are left to work out when — or whether — any of it matters. Cadence starts from the other end.

The observation

A month is not one thing. For anyone with a cycle, the second half of it is measurably different from the first — energy, sleep, appetite and mood all shift, and the complaints cluster in two predictable stretches while the other two weeks are largely uneventful.

Supplement ranges almost never reflect that. They sell you thirty identical doses of the same thing and leave the timing to you, which produces two failures at once: you take things on the days they are doing nothing, and you run out of the ones you actually needed.

Once you organise a range around timing rather than ingredients, some of the formulation problems solve themselves.

Why timing changes the formula

The clearest example is vitamin B6. It has real evidence behind it for the second half of the cycle, and at the doses that do something, taking it every day for years carries a peripheral nerve risk. Every daily capsule on this shelf quietly carries that trade-off.

Because Ease is only taken for about twelve days a month, the trade-off disappears. Same dose, a third of the exposure. The cycle-shaped range is not just tidier — it is the safer way to deliver that particular ingredient, and that is the argument for the whole architecture in one example.

Four principles

One job per formula. Nothing in the range is a twelve-ingredient blend hoping something lands. Each formula has a stretch of the month it is for and a short list of actives that earn their place in it.

Doses you can look up. No proprietary blends anywhere. If an active is on the label you can see how much is in there and check it against the research yourself, without having to trust us.

Sized to a cycle. Every box holds what one cycle uses. That is why a Flow box has five sticks in it rather than thirty, and why Ease is twenty-four capsules rather than a bottle of sixty that runs out on a different date to everything else.

Honest about timing. We say before you buy how long each formula takes, including when the answer is unflattering. Build does nothing for three to four weeks. Saying so costs us some first orders and saves the ones that would otherwise cancel in week two convinced they were sold air.

How a formula gets decided

Mostly by refusal. The first draft of any formula is longer than the last one, and the interesting work is deciding what comes out.

Ease is the clearest case. Chasteberry is the obvious ingredient for a luteal formula and nearly every competitor uses it. It is also routinely flagged for interaction with hormonal contraception — which a very large share of the people this product is for are taking. We could have included it and buried a warning, or included it and quietly lost those customers. Instead we built around it with saffron, which costs more and keeps everybody in.

Calcium came out of the same formula for a duller reason: delivering a useful amount would have pushed the dose to four capsules a day for a mineral most people already get from food. It did not earn its bulk. Melatonin never went into Rest. Senna was never a candidate. The full list, with reasons attached, is on the standards page — we publish it because what a company refuses to use tells you more than what it advertises.

Sourcing and testing

Manufactured under cGMP in an FDA-registered facility, with identity testing on incoming raw materials and independent US-lab heavy-metal screening on every production lot. Certificates are published against batch numbers so the code on your box can be matched to the paperwork for the run it came from.

Creatine gets an extra assay of its own, because it is the most counterfeited raw material in the industry and the standard panel does not look for the impurities that matter there.

What we are not

We are not a clinic and this is not medical advice. Supplements in the United States are regulated as food, which means we can describe how an ingredient supports normal body function and cannot claim it treats or prevents anything — and we think that line is correct rather than inconvenient. You will see us name symptoms, because describing them accurately is useful. You will not see us name the conditions they sometimes belong to, or promise to fix them.

If something needs diagnosing, that is a doctor's job.
