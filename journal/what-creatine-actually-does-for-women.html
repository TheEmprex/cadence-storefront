What creatine actually does for women

8 min read · Formulation notes on Build

Creatine is the most researched supplement ingredient in existence, and almost all of that research was designed around men trying to lift heavier things. That framing has been remarkably effective at hiding what it is actually useful for.

The mechanism, in one paragraph

Your cells run on ATP. ATP holds roughly a few seconds of energy for anything intense, and then it has to be regenerated. Creatine — stored in tissue as phosphocreatine — is the fastest regeneration system your body has: it donates a phosphate group and turns spent ATP back into usable ATP almost immediately.

Your body makes some on its own and you get more from meat and fish. Neither route fills the tank completely. Supplementing raises the stored amount, which extends how long the fast system can keep up before slower ones take over. That is the whole thing. Everything else is a consequence.

Why the muscle framing undersells it

The tissue most people associate with creatine is skeletal muscle, and the benefit most often measured is performance in short, hard efforts. That is real, and if you lift, it matters.

But the brain is also a high-demand tissue that runs on ATP, and it also stores creatine. Research into cognitive effects has grown considerably in recent years, and the most consistent signal is not that creatine makes a well-rested person sharper — it is that it appears most useful when the system is under strain. Sleep deprivation and demanding mental tasks are where the differences have tended to show up.

For a lot of people that is a more relevant benefit than an extra rep. It is also worth being precise about the strength of it: the cognitive literature is younger and smaller than the performance literature, and the honest framing is promising rather than settled.

There is a third strand that matters over a longer horizon. Muscle mass and bone density both decline with age, and that decline accelerates around menopause. Anything that makes strength training more productive in your twenties and thirties is compounding into that. Creatine is not a bone supplement. It is a multiplier on the thing that best protects bone.

The useful reframe: creatine is not a muscle product that women can also take. It is a cellular energy product, and muscle is the application it was first studied in.

Does the research apply to women?

Fairly asked, and the honest answer has two halves. The historical evidence base skewed heavily male, as most sports science did. Research including women has expanded meaningfully in recent years and has not found the mechanism to work differently — the phosphocreatine system is not sex-specific.

There is an argument, still being worked out, that baseline stores differ and that responses may vary across the cycle. It is interesting and it is not yet settled enough to dose around. We took the position that the standard researched amount, taken consistently, is the defensible choice, and that inventing a cycle-varying creatine protocol ahead of the evidence would be marketing rather than formulation.

Three to four weeks, and why we skipped loading

Creatine works by saturation. You are not taking a dose that acts today; you are topping up a store that fills gradually. At 5 g a day that takes roughly three to four weeks.

There is a faster route. Loading protocols use around 20 g a day for a week to reach saturation sooner, and they work. We left it out for three reasons. It quadruples the amount of product consumed in the first week, which quadruples the cost of a customer's first month. It is the main source of the stomach complaints creatine is unfairly famous for, because that much at once is a lot for the gut to handle. And it hands a new customer a bad first week of a product that is otherwise entirely unremarkable to take.

The destination is identical. Five grams daily arrives a fortnight later and nobody has a bad time on the way.

The consequence is a claim most brands would rather not make, so we make it loudly instead: Build does nothing for the first three weeks. If you judge it at day ten you will conclude it does not work, and you will be judging an empty tank. This is exactly why it ships inside a kit you keep rather than a bottle you test.

The water retention question, answered straight

Creatine draws water into muscle cells. That is not a side effect, it is adjacent to the mechanism — cells hold more creatine and more water with it.

The distinction that gets lost: this is intracellular water, inside the muscle, not subcutaneous water sitting under the skin. Those look different. Subcutaneous retention softens an outline; intracellular water fills one out. Most people notice little or nothing beyond the first fortnight, and scale weight may move by a pound or two early on before settling.

We say this before you buy rather than after, because someone who sees the scale move in week two without warning concludes something has gone wrong and stops.

Why we test our own rather than license a brand

Creatine monohydrate is a single, unpatentable molecule with a pharmacopoeial monograph. There is no proprietary version that works better. What there is, is a purity question: manufacturing can leave behind creatinine, dicyandiamide and dihydrotriazine, and creatine is among the most counterfeited raw materials in the entire supplement trade.

The usual answer is to license a branded raw material and print its logo. That roughly doubles the ingredient cost — and because the counterfeiting problem attaches to the brand itself, buying the logo through a manufacturer still leaves you needing to verify it independently. You pay the premium and do the work anyway.

So we buy quality monohydrate and commission our own third-party assay for those impurities, published against the batch number. Cheaper, and it produces a document with our lot code on it rather than someone else's badge on our box. The certificates are here.

Where it sits in the month
