The cycle

Why the second half of your month feels different

7 min read · Formulation notes on Ease

Something genuinely changes after ovulation. It is not imagination, it is not weakness, and the fact that it is so often described as either says more about who has historically been doing the describing than about the physiology.

What actually shifts

Ovulation splits the month in two. The first half — the follicular phase — runs on rising oestrogen, and most people report it as the easier stretch: energy climbing, training feeling lighter, mood steadier. The second half is the luteal phase, and its defining feature is progesterone, which rises after ovulation and then falls away sharply if no pregnancy occurs.

That rise and fall is not a background event. Progesterone and its metabolites act on the same receptor system in the brain that governs calm and sedation — the same one alcohol and anti-anxiety medication act on. Levels climb, the system adapts to them, and then over a few days they drop. The withdrawal from that adaptation is a reasonable way to understand why the last stretch before a period so often brings irritability and a shortened fuse, and why sleep that had been fine stops landing.

Progesterone also nudges body temperature up by a few tenths of a degree, which sounds trivial and is not: falling core temperature is one of the signals the body uses to initiate sleep, and a warmer baseline makes that harder. It slows gut transit, which is a large part of the bloating. It interacts with the systems governing sodium and fluid balance, which is most of the puffiness. And metabolic rate rises modestly in the luteal phase — a real increase in energy requirement, which is a more useful frame for the cravings than willpower is.

None of this is pathology. It is a functioning cycle doing what it does — which is precisely why the useful response is timing rather than treatment.

Why the complaints cluster

Ask a room of people to describe their month and the answers concentrate in two windows: the days they are bleeding, and the fortnight before. The middle two weeks generate very little commentary, because feeling normal is not memorable.

This is why the Cadence range has nothing sold specifically for the follicular and ovulatory phases. A product aimed at the weeks with no felt problem is a product sold on hope. It is also why Ease is taken for about twelve days rather than thirty — the formula is aimed at a window, not at a month.

What the ingredients are doing

Magnesium. Involved in hundreds of enzymatic processes, including several relevant to muscle relaxation and nervous system regulation, and it is one of the more commonly under-consumed minerals in a typical Western diet. Research on magnesium and premenstrual symptoms has been running for decades with mixed but broadly encouraging results — enough to justify its place, not enough to justify hyperbole. The form matters more than most labels admit: magnesium glycinate is well tolerated, while citrate and oxide have a laxative effect that is actively unhelpful in a formula meant to address bloating.

Saffron. The least expected ingredient in the formula and the one carrying the most weight. Saffron extract has been studied for mood in several contexts, including premenstrual mood symptoms, at doses around 30 mg a day — which is the amount we use. It is expensive, which is why it appears rarely, and it does not interact with hormonal contraception, which is why it appears here.

Vitamin B6. A cofactor in the synthesis of serotonin and dopamine, and the ingredient with the longest history in this space. Reviews of the evidence have generally been cautiously positive for premenstrual symptoms at moderate doses. The complication is that B6 is one of the few water-soluble vitamins with a genuine upper limit — chronic high intake over long periods is associated with peripheral nerve problems, and the doses commonly sold are not always far from that territory. More on this below, because it drove the design of the whole product.

Zinc. Involved in immune function, hormone metabolism and skin. Modest evidence in this specific context, included at a sensible amount rather than a heroic one.

The ingredient we left out

Chasteberry — vitex agnus-castus — is the obvious choice here. It has the longest traditional use, the most consumer recognition and a reasonable body of trial evidence for premenstrual symptoms. Nearly every competing product on this shelf uses it.

It is also routinely flagged for potential interaction with hormonal contraception. The proposed mechanism runs through dopaminergic activity and prolactin, and while the clinical significance is debated rather than settled, the warning appears consistently enough that responsible sources repeat it.

A very large share of the people this formula is for are on hormonal birth control. That left three options: include chasteberry and bury the warning, include it and lose those customers to a footnote at the bottom of the label, or build the formula without it. We built without it. Saffron costs several times more per dose and does a narrower job. It keeps everybody in.

Why twelve days is a safety feature

This is the part that changed how we thought about the whole range.

B6 is useful in this window at doses meaningfully above the daily requirement. It also accumulates, and prolonged high intake carries a nerve risk that is well enough established to have driven regulatory attention in several countries. Every daily premenstrual capsule on the market carries that trade-off quietly — you take the luteal-phase dose on the twenty days a month it is doing nothing, and the exposure adds up over years.

Taking it for twelve days a month instead of thirty cuts that exposure by roughly two-thirds while keeping the dose intact in the window where it matters. The cycle-shaped range is not just a tidier way to merchandise supplements. For this particular ingredient it is the safer way to deliver it, and that argument is what convinced us the architecture was worth building.

What to expect, honestly

Ease is judged over one or two full cycles, not over a week. The first cycle tells you whether anything is happening; the second tells you how much. If two cycles pass and nothing has changed, it is not for you — cancel before the third box rather than giving it another six months on the assumption that you are doing it wrong.

And the boundary worth stating plainly: this is a supplement, formulated to support normal function through a normal phase of a normal cycle. If your symptoms are severe enough to interfere with your life, that is worth a conversation with a doctor, and no capsule is a substitute for that conversation.
